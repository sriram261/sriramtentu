I turned my portfolio into a terminal

Oct 12, 2026 · 4 min read

Every developer portfolio looks the same. Hero section, skills grid, project cards, contact form. Recruiters skim dozens of them a day, and after the tenth one they all blur together. I wanted mine to be the one they remember, so I rebuilt it as something they have to touch: a working terminal.

The site opens like a machine booting up. There is a real command line where you can type help, whoami, skills, projects. There is a chatbot trained on my actual life that answers questions about my work, my companies, my research, even my thesis. There is a parking game, a drag race, a lamp you click to reveal the contact form. None of it is decoration. All of it runs.

Show, don't tell

A skills section says "I know JavaScript." A terminal that parses your input, handles typos gracefully, and answers 38 different topics about me proves I know JavaScript. Every interaction on the page is a live demo of the exact skill a client or employer is evaluating. That is the whole thesis: the portfolio is the portfolio piece.

What it taught me

First, playfulness is a filter. The people who smile at the terminal are the people I want to work with. The ones who find it unprofessional were never going to be my clients anyway.

Second, interactivity buys attention. A visitor who types three commands has spent two minutes with my work. A visitor who scrolls a static page gives it twenty seconds. Time on site is the scarcest resource on the internet, and interaction earns it.

Third, constraints force creativity. The whole site is one HTML file. No framework, no build step, no backend. Every feature had to earn its bytes, which kept the thing fast and honest.

The business angle

Here is the part nobody expects: the terminal portfolio became a product demo. When I tell a local business owner "I build websites," they nod politely. When I show them the terminal and say "imagine your customers playing with something like this," they lean in. The portfolio sells the service.
